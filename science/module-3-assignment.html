Question 1 — Two bar magnets (4 marks)

1a
The S pole of one magnet is being brought up to the N pole of the other — unlike poles attract, so the magnets pull together.
1 mark for naming the poles that meet (S and N), 1 mark for "unlike poles attract" (or for saying the magnets attract each other / pull together).
1b
Two N poles are being pushed together — like poles repel, so the magnets push apart (the double arrows show the force pushing each magnet away).
1 mark for identifying that the same poles (N and N) are facing each other, 1 mark for "like poles repel" / the magnets push apart.

Question 2 — Three rods: magnet, iron or copper (7 marks)

2a
Rod A = iron · Rod B = copper · Rod C = magnet. Missing observations: Rod B (S near dot) → nothing happens; Rod C (S near dot) → repels.
  • Rod A is attracted by both poles of the bar magnet — that's the giveaway for iron (a magnetic material that isn't itself a magnet: either pole induces magnetism and pulls it in).
  • Rod B isn't attracted by the N pole, so it cannot be a magnet or iron — it must be copper (non-magnetic). The S pole will also do nothing, so the missing observation is nothing happens.
  • Rod C is attracted by the N pole, so its dotted end must be an S pole — that means Rod C is a magnet. When the S pole of the bar magnet is brought up to the same (S) end, like poles repel, so the missing observation is repels.
Marks: 1 for each of the three rod types + 1 for each missing observation = 5.
2b
Any two of:
  • Increase the current through the coil (e.g. use a bigger voltage / extra cell).
  • Add more turns of wire to the coil.
  • Use a softer iron core (or a thicker / longer iron core).
Any two of these for 2 marks. Standard exam pair is "more current" and "more turns".

Question 3 — Nina's bicycle lamps (2 marks)

3a
Switch A goes on the front-lamp branch only — on the wire above (or below) the front lamp, between the front lamp and the battery branch.
A switch only breaks the loop it sits in. Putting it on the front-lamp's branch breaks the front lamp's circuit but leaves the rear lamp's branch intact, so the rear lamp keeps working.
3b
Switch B goes in series with the battery — on the middle branch, between the battery and where the wire splits to the two lamps.
A switch in the main "trunk" wire (the one that all current passes through) breaks the supply to every branch at once, so both lamps go off together.

Question 4 — Mary's electromagnet (5 marks)

4a
When current flows through the coil, the iron core becomes magnetised — the coil is now an electromagnet — and it attracts the iron disc downwards. That extra downward pull adds to the disc's weight, so the spring in the Newton meter stretches further and the reading goes up.
1 mark for "current makes the coil an electromagnet / magnetises the iron core"; 1 mark for "the magnet attracts the iron disc, pulling it down so the spring stretches more / the Newton meter reads a larger force".
4b
The coil would overheat — the wire (or the plastic insulation around it) could melt or burn.
Too much current means too much electrical energy turned into heat. Accept: "the wire melts", "the insulation burns/catches fire", "the coil is damaged / breaks".
4c
Any two of:
  1. As the current through the coil increases, the force on the iron disc increases (for both coils — both curves rise as current rises).
  2. The 200-turn coil produces a bigger force than the 100-turn coil at the same current — so adding more turns makes the electromagnet stronger.
  3. (Either of the above also works, or: the force rises more steeply as the current gets larger — the relationship is not a straight line.)
1 mark per conclusion, up to 2.

Question 5 — Max's circuits (8 marks)

5a
Bulb E.
Bulbs A, B, C and D sit on their own parallel branches, so removing any one of them still leaves complete paths through the others. Bulb E is in the main wire between the battery (and switch S) and the parallel set — every bit of current has to pass through E, so if E breaks the whole circuit is broken and every bulb goes off.
5b
Table of bulb states:
switch 1switch 2bulb Pbulb Qbulb R
openopenoffoffoff
openclosedonoffon
closedopenoffoffoff
Key idea: the plastic comb is an insulator — no current can flow through Q's branch even when switch 1 is closed. The metal key is a conductor — R's branch behaves like a normal wire when switch 2 is closed. Bulb P is in series with the battery, so it lights whenever any complete path exists.
  • Row 2 (sw1 open, sw2 closed): current flows battery → P → metal key → R → switch 2 → battery. P and R light; Q's branch is broken at switch 1.
  • Row 3 (sw1 closed, sw2 open): Q's branch is closed but blocked by the plastic comb (insulator); R's branch is broken at switch 2. No current flows anywhere — every bulb off.
6 marks: 1 per correct cell.
5c
A2 = 0.8 A and A3 = 0.8 A (the first row).
Circuit 3 is a series circuit — one single loop with no branches — so the current is the same at every point. Whatever A1 reads, A2 and A3 must read too. Current is not "used up" by bulbs.

Question 6 — Lemon cell (5 marks)

6a
The clock is working — it shows the time (12:30).
The clock can only run if current is flowing through it, so the lemon-with-two-metals must be producing electricity → it is a working cell.
6b
A voltmeter.
Voltage is measured in volts using a voltmeter, connected in parallel across the cell.
6c
Any one of (the control variables):
  • Use the same lemon each time (or lemons of the same size / juiciness).
  • Same size of metal strips (same length and width / surface area in the juice).
  • Same depth pushed into the lemon.
  • Same distance apart in the lemon.
  • Same temperature.
Only the pair of metals (the independent variable) should change.
6d
Magnesium and copper — 1.7 V (the biggest number in the table).
Bigger voltage = bigger "push" on the electrons. The further apart two metals are in the reactivity series, the bigger the cell voltage — and magnesium and copper are the furthest apart of the four metals used.
6e
Two of the same metal give 0 V — no voltage, so no current, so the clock won't run.
A cell needs two different metals (different reactivities) to produce a voltage. The diagonal of the table (copper-copper, iron-iron, zinc-zinc, magnesium-magnesium) is all 0 V — there is no "push" on the electrons when both electrodes are identical.

Question 7 — Paul's torch (5 marks)

7a i
Closing the switch completes the circuit, so current can flow from the battery through the bulb (and the bulb lights).
An open switch is a gap in the wire — no current can cross it. Closing the switch joins the two contacts and gives the current an unbroken loop.
7a ii
Connect the battery, switch and bulb in a single loop (series circuit) using straight wires.
7b i
The two batteries are facing each other — the same poles are touching in the middle, so the cells oppose each other. There is no overall voltage to push current round the circuit, so the bulb cannot light.
For two cells in series to work, the + of one must touch the − of the other. With like poles together, the EMFs cancel and the net voltage at the bulb is zero.
7b ii
Turn one of the batteries the other way round so the + terminal of one battery touches the − terminal of the other.
Once the cells are correctly in series, their voltages add together and current can flow through the bulb. (Equivalent answer: "put the batteries the right way round".)
7c
Paper is an insulator — it stops current crossing between the two switch contacts, so the circuit is broken (incomplete) even with the switch pressed down.
The strip is there in the box to stop the torch turning on by accident during shipping — it keeps the metal contacts apart. Once it's removed, the contacts can touch when the switch is closed and current can flow.

Question 8 — Foram's electromagnet (4 marks)

8a
Circle the point at 5 V, 27 paper clips.
Every other point sits exactly on the straight line (1 V → 5, 2 V → 10, 3 V → 15, 4 V → 20, 6 V → 30, 7 V → 34). The 5 V reading should be about 25 clips on the trend, but it's 27 — clearly above the line. That's the anomaly.
8b
The new paper clips are made from a non-magnetic metal — e.g. aluminium, copper or brass — so they are not attracted by a magnet (even a strong electromagnet).
Only iron, steel, nickel and cobalt are attracted by magnets. Ordinary steel paper clips work; aluminium ones don't.
8c
Draw at least two field lines that loop from the N pole round to the S pole on the outside of the bar, with an arrow on at least one line pointing N → S.
1 mark for at least two sensible field lines in the correct shape (curving from N round to S, not crossing); 1 mark for an arrow on at least one line in the N → S direction (outside the bar).
