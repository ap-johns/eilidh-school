About Question 1
Question 1 is the Practical 2: Population write-up — a quadrat-sampling investigation Eilidh carries out herself, so most of it depends on the organism and sites she chooses. Below are model answers for the parts that have a fixed answer plus what the examiner is looking for on the open parts.

Question 1 — Practical: sampling a population (10 marks)

1a
If you only chose the spots with the most daisies the result would be biased — too high — and wouldn't represent the whole field. Random sampling gives a fair, representative estimate of the real population.
1 mark. Accept "to avoid bias", "to make it a fair test", "so the sample represents the whole area", or "cherry-picking the best spots gives a false / too-high answer".
1b
Student's choice — the organism she counts (e.g. daisies, dandelions, clover, or lichen on a tree). Its population is the dependent variable (the thing she measures).
1 mark for naming a sensible organism that can be counted in a quadrat.
1c
Student's choice — the condition she deliberately changes between the two sites, e.g. sun vs shade, wet vs dry ground, or mown vs unmown grass. This is the independent variable.
1 mark. It must be a single environmental condition that differs between Site 1 and Site 2.
1d
Her own method (4 marks). A full-mark method covers all four prompts:
  • Sample area — how big each site is and how she measured it with the tape measure (and that both sites are the same size).
  • Quadrat size — the size of her quadrat (e.g. 0.25 m × 0.25 m) and roughly how many would fill the whole site.
  • Number of quadrats sampled at each site (more = more reliable; the sheet suggests around ten).
  • Random placement — e.g. tossing the quadrat over her shoulder, or using numbered grid coordinates picked at random.
1 mark for each of the four points addressed clearly.
1e
Her own results, filled into the table (one count per quadrat for Site 1 and Site 2).
1 mark for a sensibly completed table — whole numbers, both sites filled in, same number of quadrats for each.
1f
Add up the counts from all the quadrats at a site, then scale up to the whole area: multiply by (total area ÷ area actually sampled).
2 marks. Worked example: a 0.25 m² quadrat used 10 times samples 2.5 m². If she counts 40 daisies in total and the whole site is 25 m², the estimate is 40 × (25 ÷ 2.5) = 400 daisies. (The sheet's simpler version: ten 1 m² quadrats cover 10 m², so multiply the total count by 10 to estimate a 100 m² field.) 1 mark for adding the counts, 1 mark for scaling up correctly.

Question 2 — Photosynthesis (5 marks)

2a
Water + carbon dioxide → glucose (starch) + oxygen.
1 mark for carbon dioxide, 1 mark for oxygen. (Full reaction: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen, using light energy.)
2b
Energy = light (sunlight) · Pigment = chlorophyll.
1 mark each. Chlorophyll is the green pigment in the chloroplasts that absorbs the light energy.
2c
Stomata are tiny pores (mainly on the underside of the leaf) that let carbon dioxide diffuse in for photosynthesis (and oxygen and water vapour out).
1 mark for "let carbon dioxide in / allow gas exchange". Bonus context: they can open and close to control water loss.

Question 3 — Waterweed and light (5 marks)

3a i
Beaker C (bright light).
1 mark. More light = a faster rate of photosynthesis, and beaker C has the most light.
3a ii
There was no light inside the black box, so the waterweed couldn't photosynthesise — its chlorophyll broke down, so it lost its green colour and turned pale yellow.
1 mark for linking "no light → can't photosynthesise / chlorophyll lost". Accept "it couldn't make food in the dark".
3b
The large lily leaves on the surface block out the light, so plants below the surface don't get enough light to photosynthesise (and grow).
1 mark. Key idea is competition for light — the lilies shade everything beneath them.
3c i
The waterweed in beaker E (with fertiliser) grows better / bigger / faster than in beaker D.
1 mark. The fertiliser supplies extra minerals (nitrates) that the plant needs for healthy growth; beaker D is the control.
3c ii
Minerals.
1 mark. (Not fat, sand or sugar.) Fertilisers provide mineral salts such as nitrates for growth.

Question 4 — Bindweed (4 marks)

4a i
… as much light as possible.
1 mark. Climbing high gets the bindweed up above its neighbours into the light for photosynthesis.
4a ii
… the rose bush provides support for the bindweed.
1 mark. The bindweed has a weak stem, so it uses the rose bush to hold itself up.
4b
No water.
1 mark. Cutting the stem at X breaks the path that carries water (and minerals) up from the roots, so the part above X dries out and dies. (Not air, warmth or light — those still reach it.)
4c
Minerals.
1 mark. Plants take up mineral salts (e.g. nitrates) from fertiliser through their roots — not acids, sugars or vitamins.

Question 5 — Woodland food chain (4 marks)

5a i
oak tree → caterpillar → blackbird → owl.
1 mark for the chain in the correct order. Built from the clues: caterpillars eat oak leaves, blackbirds eat caterpillars, owls eat blackbirds. (The arrows point in the direction the energy flows — from the food to the feeder.)
5a ii
It makes food by photosynthesis.
1 mark. A producer makes its own food (so it starts the food chain). The other ticks are true facts about oak trees but don't define a producer.
5b i
The number of orange-striped caterpillars decreased (went down).
1 mark.
5b ii
The two types of caterpillar compete for the same food (the oak leaves). As the gypsy moth caterpillars increased they ate more of the leaves, leaving less food for the orange-striped caterpillars.
1 mark. The key word the examiner wants is competition — for the same limited food source.

Question 6 — DDT in a food chain (3 marks)

6a
DDT is not broken down or excreted, so it stays in the body and is passed along the food chain. Each animal eats many of the organisms below it, so the DDT builds up and gets more concentrated at each level — the heron (top of the chain) ends up with the most.
1 mark for any one reason: "it builds up / accumulates along the chain", "it isn't broken down or removed from the body", or "each level eats lots of the one below". This is bioaccumulation.
6b i
Before birth: it passes from the mother's blood to the pup through the placenta.
1 mark. Accept "across the placenta" or "through the mother's blood / the cord".
6b ii
After birth: through the mother's milk (when the pup suckles / feeds).
1 mark.

Question 7 — Antarctic food web (5 marks)

7a
Any two of: squid, cod, crabeater seal.
1 mark. These three have only one arrow coming into them — from krill — so krill is their only food. The penguin doesn't count: it eats krill and squid.
7b i
Producers.
1 mark. Plants make their own food by photosynthesis, so they are producers.
7b ii
Herbivores.
1 mark. Krill eat the tiny plants (and nothing else here), so they are plant-eaters — herbivores.
7c i
The number of crabeater seals would decrease (fall).
1 mark. Krill is the crabeater seal's only food, so fewer krill means less food and fewer seals.
7c ii
Crabeater seals eat only krill, so a drop in krill hits them straight away. Leopard seals eat several different animals (penguins, squid, cod, crabeater seals), so they still have other food and are affected later.
1 mark. The point is that leopard seals have alternative prey, whereas crabeater seals depend on krill alone.

Question 8 — Woodland light & respiration (5 marks)

8a
The trees grow their leaves in May — the leafy canopy fills out and blocks (absorbs) the light, so less reaches the woodland floor.
1 mark. Link the new leaves to shading the floor.
8b
More light means more photosynthesis, so the plants make more glucose (food) for growth — so they grow bigger and faster.
2 marks: 1 for "more light → more photosynthesis", 1 for "more food / glucose made → more growth".
8c
oxygen + glucose → carbon dioxide + water.
2 marks: 1 for glucose, 1 for water. (Respiration is essentially photosynthesis in reverse.)
