Question 1 — Practical 3: Insulation (12 marks)

1a
Independent variable: the type of insulating material.
Dependent variable: the temperature of the water (i.e. the rate of heat loss).
2 marks — 1 each. Independent = the thing you deliberately change; dependent = the thing you measure as a result.
1b
To make it a fair test. If the amount of material or volume of water changed too, you couldn't tell whether the type of material caused the difference in heat loss, or the different amount.
1 mark. These are the control variables — kept the same so only the independent variable affects the result.
1c
The control (control experiment).
1 mark. The cup with no material gives a baseline to compare the insulated cups against — it shows how fast the water cools with no insulation at all.
1d · table
A results table with time down the side (0–10 minutes) and a temperature column for each material plus one for no material.
3 marks. Full marks for: a ruled table; column headings with units — e.g. "Time (min)" and "Temperature (°C)"; and a row for every minute from 0 to 10. Example layout:
Time (min) | Material 1 (°C) | Material 2 (°C) | No material (°C)
1e · graph
A line graph: time on the horizontal axis, temperature on the vertical axis, with a separate curve (line of best fit) for each material and for no material.
5 marks. Full marks for: axes labelled with units; a sensible scale that fills the grid; points plotted accurately; a smooth line of best fit for each set. The curve that falls most slowly is the best insulator; the "no material" curve should fall fastest.

Question 2 — Luke heats different volumes of water (7 marks)

2a
So he can work out the temperature rise (end − start). The rise is what he is really investigating, and you can only find it if you know both temperatures.
1 mark.
2b
Yes. The smaller the volume of water, the bigger the temperature rise: 25 cm³ rose 12 °C, 50 cm³ rose 6 °C, 75 cm³ rose only 4 °C. So the rise does depend on the volume, as he predicted.
1 mark. Quote the figures from the table (rises of 12, 6 and 4 °C) to back up the "yes".
2c
Stirring spreads the thermal energy evenly through the water, so the thermometer reads the temperature of all the water, not just a hot patch near the flame.
1 mark. This makes each reading accurate and the results reliable.
2d
✓ "The same amount of energy went into all three beakers."
1 mark. Same candle for the same time = same energy supplied to each. The smallest volume (A) just gets hotter because that energy is shared between fewer water particles.
2e
It was transferred to the surroundings (the air and the bench). The energy is spread out / dissipated — not destroyed.
1 mark. Energy is conserved: it moves from the hot water to the cooler surroundings until they are at the same temperature.
2f
Work out the mean (average) of the three sets of results for each beaker. Yes, it makes the results more reliable.
2 marks — 1 for "calculate the mean", 1 for explaining that repeating and averaging reduces the effect of random errors and shows up any anomalies.

Question 3 — Energy used by four appliances (5 marks)

3a
5% of the light bulb's energy is given out as light.
1 mark. 100% − 95% wasted = 5% useful (light).
3b
60% of the mixer's energy is wasted.
1 mark. 100% − 40% useful (moving the whisks) = 60% wasted.
3c
…some of the electrical energy is changed into thermal (heat) energy which is wasted.
1 mark. Friction in the moving parts turns some electrical energy into heat.
3d
The radio — sound is its useful output, so that sound is not wasted.
1 mark. (In the other appliances any sound produced is wasted energy.)
3e
The light bulb — it wastes 95%, the highest of the four.
1 mark. Compare: kettle 2%, radio 50%, mixer 60%, light bulb 95%.

Question 4 — Mobile phone energy transfers (2 marks)

4a
✓ electrical → chemical.
1 mark. Charging stores energy in the battery as chemical energy.
4b
✓ chemical → electrical → sound.
1 mark. The battery's chemical energy becomes electrical energy, which the speaker turns into sound.

Question 5 — Log car theme-park ride (8 marks)

5a i
A and F.
1 mark (both needed). No kinetic energy means not moving — the car is stationary at the start (A) and after it hits the bumper at the finish (F).
5a ii
A.
1 mark. Most gravitational potential energy = the highest point, which is the start, A.
5a iii
E.
1 mark. E is the lowest point the car reaches while still moving (in the water trench), so it has the least GPE but still some kinetic energy. (F is just as low but the car has stopped there.)
5b i
Gravity (the car's weight pulling it downhill).
1 mark. From B to C the track goes downhill, so gravity speeds the car up.
5b ii
Friction / water resistance (drag).
1 mark. Pushing through the water is a resistive force that slows the car down.
5c
When the car hits the bumper at F, its kinetic energy is transferred into sound energy and thermal energy.
3 marks — 1 per correct word (kinetic → sound + thermal). The impact makes a noise (sound) and warms the bumper and car slightly (thermal).

Question 6 — Solar panel (5 marks)

6a i
Because the amount of sunlight changes during the day — it is weak at dawn and dusk and strongest around midday when the Sun is highest in the sky.
1 mark. More light energy hits the panel the higher and brighter the Sun is.
6a ii
About 8 hours.
1 mark. On the June graph the output is at or above 0.7 kJ/s roughly from 10 am to 6 pm — read where the curve crosses the 0.7 line on the way up and on the way down, then find the gap (≈ 8 hours). Accept 7–9 hours read carefully off the graph.
6b i · curve
Draw a curve of the same shape but lower and narrower: a smaller peak (less energy in winter) that starts later in the morning and ends earlier in the evening (shorter daylight).
2 marks — 1 for a lower peak, 1 for a narrower curve (shorter day). It should still peak around noon.
6b ii
15° per hour.
1 mark. 7 am to 7 pm is 12 hours; 180° ÷ 12 = 15° each hour.

Question 7 — Copper rod expands when heated (4 marks)

7a i
The atoms vibrate more (faster, with bigger movements) — they gain kinetic energy.
1 mark.
7a ii
The average distance between the atoms increases (they move slightly further apart), so the rod gets longer.
1 mark. This is why the rod expands when heated.
7b i
About 220 °C.
1 mark. A 1.0 mm increase means a length of 301.0 mm. Read across from 301.0 mm on the graph to the line, then down to the temperature axis ≈ 220 °C.
7b ii
299.9 mm.
1 mark. The line falls by about 0.1 mm between room temperature (20 °C) and 0 °C, so from 300.0 mm it drops to ≈ 299.9 mm (the rod is slightly shorter when colder). Read it off by extending the line back to 0 °C.

Question 8 — Energy transfer processes (4 marks)

8a
radiation → "The way energy from the Sun reaches the Earth."
convection → "The way the air above a heater becomes warm."
1 mark. (The third example — the metal handle getting hot — is conduction, which isn't one of the two methods offered.)
8c
Heat travels through the copper rod by conduction. The particles at the heated end gain energy and vibrate more. They collide with neighbouring particles and pass the energy along, particle to particle, down the rod. In a metal, free electrons also carry the energy quickly from the hot end to the cold end, which is why metals conduct so well.
3 marks — roughly: 1 for particles at the hot end vibrating more/gaining kinetic energy; 1 for energy passed on by collisions between neighbouring particles; 1 for free (delocalised) electrons carrying energy through the metal.
