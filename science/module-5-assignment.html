The reactivity series for this assignment
Question 1 gives this order, most to least reactive: potassium · sodium · magnesium · aluminium · iron · lead · copper. The golden rule for everything below: a more reactive metal pushes a less reactive metal out of its compound (displacement), and the more reactive a metal, the more keenly it reacts with acid and the harder it is to find as the pure metal in nature.

Question 1 — The reactivity series (5 marks)

1a
Magnesium is more reactive than copper, so the magnesium takes the place of (displaces) the copper in the copper sulphate, leaving copper metal behind.
1 mark. The key word is displacement — the more reactive metal pushes the less reactive one out of its compound.
1b
iron + sodium chloride → No. Iron is less reactive than sodium, so it cannot displace sodium.
magnesium + lead nitrate → Yes. Magnesium is more reactive than lead, so it displaces the lead.
2 marks — 1 for each correct yes/no with a reason that compares their positions in the series. A "yes/no" with no reason scores 0 for that row.
1c i
Add zinc to solutions of the other metals' salts (e.g. copper sulphate, lead nitrate, magnesium sulphate, iron sulphate) and see which ones it displaces the metal from.
1 mark. Accept "do displacement reactions with the salt solutions of the other metals", or "react zinc and each of the other metals with the same acid and compare how vigorously they fizz".
1c ii
Zinc goes above every metal it manages to displace (push out of solution), and below any metal that displaces it. That fixes its position in the series.
1 mark. Equivalent answer for the acid method: the more vigorously zinc reacts compared with the others, the higher up it goes.

Question 2 — Metal wires in salt solutions (6 marks)

2a
most reactive → zinc → lead → copper → silver ← least reactive.
2 marks for the fully correct order. Read from the results: copper displaced silver (copper > silver); zinc displaced lead (zinc > lead); copper did not displace lead (copper < lead). Put together: zinc > lead > copper > silver.
2b
Zinc.
1 mark. Zinc is the most reactive of the four, so it is the only one reactive enough to react with the dilute hydrochloric acid (copper, lead and silver are too low in the series to react visibly).
2c
No — his prediction was wrong. Zinc is more reactive than silver, so it displaces the silver from silver nitrate (silver forms on the zinc wire). A reaction does happen.
1 mark for "no" with the reasoning that zinc is more reactive than silver.
2d
At the very bottom — below silver, as the least reactive metal. Because gold is so unreactive it does not combine with other elements, so it is found in nature as the pure metal.
2 marks — 1 for placing it at the bottom/below silver, 1 for linking "unreactive → doesn't combine → found as the native/pure metal".

Question 3 — Jessica investigates rusting (7 marks)

3a
Test-tube B: boiling the water drove out the dissolved oxygen (air), and rusting needs oxygen as well as water.
Test-tube C: the chemical absorbed the water vapour, so the air was dry — no water present, and rusting needs water as well as oxygen.
2 marks — 1 for each tube. Rusting needs both water and oxygen; B removes the oxygen, C removes the water.
3b i
Acidic.
1 mark.
3b ii
Hydrogen.
1 mark. A metal reacting with an acid always gives off hydrogen gas (metal + acid → salt + hydrogen).
3c i
The total mass was greater than the mass of the nail at the start (it had increased).
1 mark.
3c ii
The iron combined with oxygen to form iron oxide (rust). The extra oxygen that joined on adds to the mass, so the nail + rust weighs more.
1 mark. The point is that mass is gained from the oxygen taken in, not lost.
3d
The nail in sea water (D) — which contains salt — rusted the most, while the nail in distilled water (A) — no salt — only rusted slightly. The salt is the only difference between them, so the salt must have made it rust faster.
1 mark for comparing D with A and pinning the faster rusting on the salt being the only difference.

Question 4 — Heating carbon with lead oxide (5 marks)

4a i
Test it for a metal property — e.g. see if it conducts electricity (or conducts heat, is shiny when polished, or is malleable / can be hammered into shape without shattering).
1 mark for any one valid test of a metal property.
4a ii
Lead.
1 mark. The carbon took the oxygen from the lead oxide, leaving the lead metal behind.
4b i
Oxygen.
1 mark. Carbon dioxide is carbon combined with oxygen.
4b ii
From the lead oxide — the carbon took (removed) the oxygen from it.
1 mark. This is why the lead oxide turns into lead: carbon is more reactive than lead, so it pulls the oxygen away.
4c
Wear safety goggles / eye protection (or: don't breathe in the fumes — lead is toxic; tie hair back; let the apparatus cool before touching).
1 mark for any one sensible precaution. (The fume cupboard is already given in the question, so name a different one.)

Question 6 — Skateboard materials (11 marks)

6a i
Poly(ethene).
1 mark. Poly(ethene) is a polymer — a long-chain plastic.
6a ii
Aluminium.
1 mark. Aluminium is an element (and metals conduct electricity). Steel doesn't count — it is an alloy/mixture, not an element.
6b
all the atoms in a composite are the same — False
one example of a composite is glass reinforced plastic — True
the properties of a composite are a combination of the properties of the materials used to make it — True
materials used to make composites are always arranged in layers — False
2 marks — roughly 1 mark per two correct ticks (all four right for full marks). A composite is a mixture of different materials, so its atoms aren't all the same and it isn't always layered.
6c i
Ceramic bearings are lighter than steel, they don't rust, and they don't get hot and expand at high speed — so the wheels won't stick.
2 marks for any two clear advantages drawn from Ami's description of steel's drawbacks (lighter / don't rust / don't expand & stick when hot).
6c ii
✓ do not melt when heated  ·  ✓ hard.
2 marks (1 per correct tick). Ceramics cannot be drawn into wires, do not conduct electricity, and are not flexible — so those three are wrong.
6c iii
glass and cement.
2 marks. Ionic is a type of bond, melamine is a polymer, and nylon is a polymer — none are ceramics.
6d
The different materials (wood, glue, metal, plastic, ceramic) are hard to separate from each other, so recycling them is difficult and expensive.
1 mark for any sensible disadvantage — e.g. separating the mixed materials is hard, or it costs more energy/money than it's worth.

Question 7 — Chen investigates rusting (5 marks)

7a i
The amount (mass) of salt added to the water.
1 mark. This is the independent variable — the thing he deliberately changes.
7a ii
Any one, e.g. the volume of water (also accept: size/type of nail, temperature, time left, type of beaker).
1 mark for any sensible control variable — something kept the same so the test is fair.
7a iii
The more salt that is added, the more (and faster) the iron rusts.
1 mark for a conclusion that links increasing salt to increasing rusting (matching his results, 0 g → small rust up to 3 g → very rusty).
7b i
Oxygen.
1 mark. The dissolved oxygen in the water is what reacts with the iron.
7b ii
Rusting needs both water and oxygen. With no water in the beaker, the iron cannot rust.
1 mark. The key point is that water is missing (and the sealed beaker keeps fresh moist air out).
