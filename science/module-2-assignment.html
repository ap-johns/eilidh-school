Question 1 — Practical: solubility (11 marks)

Dissolve sugar into 100 g of water at different temperatures and record the maximum mass that dissolves.

1a
Independent variable: temperature of the water (°C).
The one you choose to change between each trial.
1b
Dependent variable: mass of sugar that dissolves (g) — the solubility.
The one you measure each time.
1c
Controls (any 2 for 2 marks):
  • Same mass / volume of water each time (100 g / 100 ml).
  • Same type of sugar (same brand, same grain size).
  • Same cup / container (same shape and insulation).
  • Same stirring — speed and time before deciding "no more dissolves".
  • Same person judging when the sugar stops dissolving.
1d
Things a high-tech lab could do (any one):
Set the temperature to an exact value with a thermostat / water bath; weigh the sugar on a balance accurate to 0.001 g; stir at a constant speed with a magnetic stirrer; run the experiment in a sealed container so no water evaporates; repeat the experiment many times and average the results.
1e
Table headings:
Left column: Temperature of water (°C). Right column: Mass of sugar dissolved (g).
Units always go in the heading, not next to every value.
1f
Graph: smooth curve sloping upward.
Temperature on the x-axis, solubility on the y-axis. Regular scale (e.g. 10 °C per 2 cm; 20 g per 2 cm). Points marked with ×. Draw a single line of best fit — a smooth curve through the trend, not dot-to-dot. Expect roughly: cold tap water ~20 g per 100 g → boiling water ~ around 200 g per 100 g (sugar is very soluble in hot water).

Question 2 — Elements, symbols and metals (9 marks)

2a
Name → symbol (4 marks):
  • neon → Ne
  • sodium → Na
  • nitrogen → N
  • aluminium → Al
Argon (Ar) and sulfur (S) are distractors.
2b
Metals: aluminium and sodium.
Neon and nitrogen are non-metals (right of the staircase on the periodic table).
2c
Three properties of metals (any three for 3 marks):
  • Shiny when polished (lustrous).
  • Good conductor of heat and electricity.
  • Malleable — can be hammered into shape.
  • Ductile — can be pulled into wires.
  • High density.
  • High melting point; usually solid at room temperature (mercury is the exception).
  • Make a ringing sound when struck (sonorous).

Question 3 — Copper heated in air (2 marks)

3a
Product: copper oxide.
Copper reacts with oxygen from the air: copper + oxygen → copper oxide. The black coating on the heated metal is copper(II) oxide.
3b
"The atoms have combined in a different way to make a new substance."
That's the defining feature of a chemical change — atoms rearrange into a new compound and it can't easily be reversed. The other options can be true of physical changes too (a solid product, needing heat, or keeping the same atoms).

Question 4 — Lithium reactions (8 marks)

4a
"The surface changes from shiny to dull."
Freshly cut lithium is shiny; it reacts with oxygen in the air to form a dull grey layer of lithium oxide on the surface. No flame, no bubbles, and the piece barely changes size.
4b
lithium + oxygen → lithium oxide
Reactants on the left of the arrow, product on the right. (1 mark for the correct reactants, 1 mark for the correct product and arrow.)
4c
"The lithium moves around" and "The lithium fizzes and bubbles form."
Lithium floats and skates across the surface, with hydrogen gas bubbling off. (The lithium gets smaller, not bigger; it doesn't sink; the water level doesn't visibly rise.)
4d
Gas: hydrogen.
Group 1 metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen. Test: a lit splint gives a "squeaky pop".
4e
Sodium melting point ≈ 97 °C.
Going down Group 1, melting point decreases: Li 180 °C → Na ? → K 64 °C. Sodium must be between those two values. Of the options given, only 97 °C fits between 64 and 180. (Real value is 98 °C.)
4f
Reacts most slowly with water: lithium.
Reactivity increases down Group 1, so lithium (top) is the least reactive of Li, Na, K — fizzes gently. Sodium melts and skates. Potassium ignites with a lilac flame.

Question 5 — Group 7 halogens (5 marks)

5a
Appearance at room temperature:
  • chlorine → green gas
  • bromine → red-brown liquid
  • iodine → grey solid
Going down the group, state changes from gas → liquid → solid (melting and boiling points rise). Grey gas, red-brown solid and green liquid are distractors.
5b
sodium + chlorine → sodium chloride
Group 1 metal + Group 7 non-metal → ionic salt (the "chloride" name comes from chlorine). 1 mark for reactants, 1 mark for product.

Question 6 — Separation apparatus (5 marks)

Apparatus A is a round-bottomed flask with a side-arm condenser leading to a collection vessel. B is an open beaker of solution with arrows showing evaporation. C is a funnel with filter paper, dripping into a beaker.

6a
A → distillation · B → crystallisation · C → filtration.
  • A: heat boils the solvent → vapour travels into a condenser → cools back to a pure liquid in the receiver. That's distillation.
  • B: open dish with arrows leaving the surface — the solvent evaporates, leaving the solute behind as crystals: crystallisation.
  • C: funnel + filter paper trapping solid, with liquid dripping through: filtration.
Chromatography is the distractor — its apparatus is paper in a beaker of solvent, not shown here.
6b
Sand from salt water → C (filtration).
Sand is insoluble — it stays on the filter paper as the residue, while the salty water passes through as the filtrate.
6c
Pure water from salt water → A (distillation).
Salt is dissolved, so filtering wouldn't separate it. Boil the solution: water evaporates and is condensed back to pure liquid in the collection vessel; the salt stays behind in the flask. (Crystallisation would give you the salt, not the water.)

Question 7 — Distillation of water-soluble ink (7 marks)

7a
"Evaporation then condensation."
The water in the ink evaporates when heated (liquid → gas), then in the cold condenser it condenses back (gas → liquid). Melting doesn't happen — there's no solid involved.
7b
Colourless liquid in the test tube: water.
The dye stays behind in the flask. Only the pure solvent (water) evaporates, condenses and drips into the test tube.
7c
Thermometer reading: 100 °C.
The thermometer measures the temperature of the vapour leaving the flask. Pure water boils at 100 °C, and that temperature stays fixed while water is boiling off — sharp, flat boiling point.
7d
Water at Y: warmer than 15 °C (e.g. ~25–35 °C).
As hot vapour condenses inside the condenser, it gives up heat to the cold water in the jacket. The jacket water absorbs that heat and warms up by the time it leaves at Y.
7e
Two changes to the water vapour in the glass tube:
  1. It cools down (temperature falls as heat is transferred to the cold-water jacket).
  2. It changes state from gas back to liquid (condenses).
7f
Why apparatus A is better than B:
Apparatus A has a jacket of constantly-flowing cold water wrapped around the inner tube (counter-flow: cold in at X, warmer water out at Y). The vapour is in contact with a long, cold surface, so it condenses much more efficiently.

In apparatus B, the glass tube only dips into a static beaker of water. The beaker water warms up quickly with no replenishment, the cooling surface is small, and a lot of vapour escapes without condensing.

Question 8 — Chromatography of inks (4 marks)

8a
Colours in the felt-tip ink: pink and red.
The felt-tip column shows two spots, at the same heights as the pink spot in the purple-ink column and a red spot above all the others. Those are the only dyes present.
8b
Green ink contains 2 coloured substances.
There are two spots above the baseline in the green-ink column (dark blue and yellow). Each spot is one separated dye — so two spots = two dyes mixed together to make green.
8c
Use a pencil.
Pencil graphite is insoluble in water — it stays where you draw it. A felt-tip line would dissolve in the solvent and run up the paper with the inks, ruining the experiment.
8d
The dyes in the second set of pens were insoluble in water but soluble in ethanol.
Chromatography only works if the substances dissolve in the solvent so they can be carried up the paper. Different solvents dissolve different things — if the inks won't dissolve in water, they don't move; swapping to ethanol gives a solvent they can dissolve in, so they separate normally.
