9.1 The percentages trick — x% of y = y% of x

The order doesn't matter, because x% of y and y% of x both equal (x × y) ÷ 100. Swap them round so you get an easy percentage to work out.

Worked example — 4% of 75

Working out 4% directly is fiddly. Swap it:

4% of 75 = 75% of 4
75% of 4 = ¾ × 4 = 3

How to use it

Swap so the percentage becomes something friendly — 50%, 25%, 10% or 1%. For example 8% of 50 = 50% of 8 = 4.

9.2 Writing a recurring decimal as a fraction

A recurring decimal has a digit or block of digits that repeats forever, e.g. 0.3333… (3 recurring) or 0.2727… (27 recurring).

Method

1. Call the decimal x.
2. Multiply by 10, 100, 1000… so the multiplier has as many zeros as the repeating block is long (one digit → ×10, two digits → ×100).
3. Subtract the original x — the repeating tail cancels.
4. Solve for x and simplify.

Worked example — 0.3333… (one repeating digit)

x = 0.3333…
10x = 3.3333…
10x − x = 3
9x = 3
x = 3/9 = 1/3

Worked example — 0.2727… (two repeating digits)

x = 0.2727…
100x = 27.2727…
100x − x = 27
99x = 27
x = 27/99 = 3/11

Terminating or recurring?

Put the fraction in its simplest form and look at the denominator. If its only prime factors are 2 and/or 5, the decimal terminates (e.g. 3/8 = 0.375, since 8 = 2×2×2). Any other prime factor (3, 7, 11…) means it recurs (e.g. 1/6 = 0.1666…, since 6 = 2×3).

9.3 Plane symmetry of 3D shapes

A plane of symmetry is a flat "mirror" that slices a 3D shape into two halves that are exact mirror images of each other. It's the 3D version of a line of symmetry.

ShapePlanes of symmetry
Cube9
Cuboid (not a cube)3
Triangular prism (equilateral)4
Square-based pyramid4
Cylinderinfinitely many
Sphereinfinitely many
Watch out

A plane of symmetry is a flat surface, not a line. Each one must cut the shape into two identical mirror halves — check by imagining folding one half onto the other.

9.4 Plans and elevations

These are three flat 2D views of a solid, usually drawn to scale on squared paper:

Plan
The view looking straight down from above.
Front elevation
The view looking at the shape from the front.
Side elevation
The view looking at the shape from the side.
Worked example — cuboid 4 cm (wide) × 2 cm (deep) × 3 cm (tall)

Plan (from above): rectangle 4 × 2
Front elevation: rectangle 4 × 3
Side elevation: rectangle 2 × 3

Tip

Line the three views up: the front elevation and plan share the same width; the side elevation and plan share the same depth. Draw to scale and keep the squares consistent.

9.5 Time series

A time series graph plots a quantity as it changes over time. Time always goes on the horizontal axis, and the points are joined with straight lines.

Reading one

Describe the overall trend — is it rising, falling or staying level? Look for repeating (seasonal) patterns, and read values off the axes rather than guessing. Don't read too much into a single point.

9.6 Interpreting stem-and-leaf diagrams

A stem-and-leaf diagram sorts data while keeping every digit. The stem is the leading digit(s) — usually the tens — and each leaf is the final digit. Leaves are written in order, smallest first, and there must always be a key.

Worked example

Stem | Leaf
 2   | 3  5  8
 3   | 1  1  4  6
 4   | 0  2
Key: 2 | 3 means 23

The nine values are 23, 25, 28, 31, 31, 34, 36, 40, 42.

Median = middle (5th) value = 31
Mode = most common = 31
Range = 42 − 23 = 19

Watch out

Read a value by joining the stem to its leaf (stem 4, leaf 2 → 42). To find the median, count the leaves in order — a diagram with 9 leaves has its median as the 5th leaf.

Quick reference

  • x% of y = y% of x — swap to get an easy percentage
  • Recurring → fraction: ×10 (or ×100), subtract, solve, simplify
  • Terminates if the simplified denominator has only 2s and 5s; otherwise recurs
  • Plane of symmetry cuts a solid into two mirror halves (cube 9, cuboid 3)
  • Plan = from above, front & side elevations = from the front and side
  • Time series: time on the x-axis; describe the trend
  • Stem-and-leaf: stem = tens, leaf = units, always give a key
