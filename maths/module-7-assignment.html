Question 1 — Name the 3D objects (6 marks)

1
cube → a  ·  cuboid → f  ·  sphere → b  ·  cone → c  ·  triangular prism → e  ·  cylinder → d
1 mark each. a is the cube (six square faces), f the cuboid (rectangular box), b the ball/sphere, c the cone, e the triangular prism (triangle cross-section), d the cylinder (circular ends).

Question 2 — Number the die net (3 marks)

2
Number the faces so each pair of opposite faces adds up to 7: the pairs are 1 & 6, 2 & 5 and 3 & 4.
3 marks — 1 per correct opposite pair. The trick is spotting which faces end up opposite when the net folds: in a straight strip of squares, a face is opposite the one two squares along from it (with one face between them). Put 1 and 6 on one such pair, 2 and 5 on another, 3 and 4 on the last. Any arrangement works as long as every opposite pair totals 7.

Question 3 — Cuboid 3 cm wide × 4 cm high × 5 cm long (11 marks)

3a · net
A net of six rectangles: two 5×4 (front & back), two 5×3 (top & bottom) and two 4×3 (the two ends).
3 marks. Easiest as a cross: draw a 5×4 rectangle in the middle, add a 5×3 above and a 5×3 below it, put a 4×3 on the left and a 4×3 on the right, then one more 5×4 joined on (e.g. below the bottom 5×3). Count the squares to get the sizes right — 1 square = 1 cm.
3b
6 faces, 8 vertices, 12 edges.
3 marks. Every cuboid has 6 faces, 8 corners (vertices) and 12 edges. (Check with Euler: 6 + 8 − 12 = 2 ✓.)
3c
94 cm²
SA = 2(lw + lh + wh) with l = 5, w = 3, h = 4:
= 2(5×3 + 5×4 + 3×4)
= 2(15 + 20 + 12) = 2 × 47 = 94 cm².
3d
60 cm³
V = length × width × height = 5 × 3 × 4 = 60 cm³.

Question 4 — Does tripling the sides triple the volume? (2 marks)

4
No — Tafara is wrong. The larger cuboid has 27 times the volume, so the smaller one must be multiplied by 27.
Original: 5 × 4 × 3 = 60 cm³.
Tripled sides: 15 × 12 × 9 = 1620 cm³.
1620 ÷ 60 = 27. Tripling every side multiplies the volume by 3 × 3 × 3 = 3³ = 27, not 3.

Question 5 — Is the triangle right-angled? (2 marks)

5
No, it is not right-angled.
Test Pythagoras with the two shorter sides against the longest (c = 6):
a² + b² = 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25
c² = 6² = 36
Since 25 ≠ 36, the squares on the two shorter sides do not add up to the square on the longest side, so the triangle is not right-angled.

Question 6 — Distance between points (6 marks)

6a
AC ≈ 5.83
Make a right-angled triangle with the horizontal and vertical gaps as the legs.
Across: 4 − 1 = 3. Up: 6 − 1 = 5.
AC² = 3² + 5² = 9 + 25 = 34
AC = √34 = 5.830… ≈ 5.83.
6b
DE ≈ 8.06
Across: 5 − (−2) = 7. Up: 2 − (−2) = 4.
DE² = 7² + 4² = 49 + 16 = 65
DE = √65 = 8.062… ≈ 8.06.

Question 7 — Journeys (6 marks)

7a
≈ 134.16 miles
The east and south legs are at right angles, so the direct route back is the hypotenuse:
d² = 120² + 60² = 14400 + 3600 = 18000
d = √18000 = 134.164… ≈ 134.16 miles.
7b
≈ 17.66 nM north
The 25 nM sailed is the hypotenuse; the 17.7 nM east is one leg. The distance north is the other leg:
north² = 25² − 17.7² = 625 − 313.29 = 311.71
north = √311.71 = 17.655… ≈ 17.66 nM.

Question 8 — Triangle PQR, right-angled at Q (4 marks)

8a · sketch
A right-angled triangle with the right angle at Q, PQ = 9.5 m and QR = 6 m as the two sides meeting at Q, and PR as the hypotenuse opposite Q.
Label the right angle at Q with a small square. PQ and QR are the two shorter sides; PR is the longest side.
8b
≈ 26.74 m
First find the hypotenuse PR:
PR² = 9.5² + 6² = 90.25 + 36 = 126.25
PR = √126.25 = 11.236… ≈ 11.24 m.
Perimeter = PQ + QR + PR = 9.5 + 6 + 11.24 = 26.74 m.
